William Randolph Hearst (*New York Journal*) and Joseph Pulitzer (*New York World*) spent years running sensational coverage of Spanish "atrocities" in Cuba — some real, many exaggerated, some entirely fabricated. The two papers competed for circulation by escalating each other's claims, locking each other into ever more lurid front pages.

When the USS *Maine* exploded in Havana Harbor on February 15, 1898, both papers blamed Spain within days, despite no investigative evidence — the probable cause was an internal coal-bunker fire. Massive front-page color illustrations, fabricated interview dialogue, undercover correspondents, and the rallying slogan "Remember the Maine, to hell with Spain!" did the rest. Hearst's reputed (likely apocryphal but era-defining) telegram to illustrator Frederic Remington — "You furnish the pictures, I'll furnish the war" — captures the operational stance precisely.

The Spanish-American War followed within weeks. The episode is the canonical case of yellow journalism delivering not just circulation gains but a national foreign policy outcome — the modern template for war agitation by a privately owned mass press.
