Reporter Richard Adams Locke, writing for Benjamin Day's *New York Sun*, ran a six-part serial claiming that astronomer Sir John Herschel had observed bat-winged humanoids ("Vespertilio-homo"), unicorns, and bipedal beavers on the Moon through a giant new telescope at the Cape of Good Hope. The story was falsely attributed to a real scientist via a nonexistent *Edinburgh Journal of Science* supplement.

The technique was deceptively simple: borrow the credibility of a real, internationally famous astronomer, cite a plausible-sounding scholarly source, and serialize the revelation across multiple issues so readers had to buy the *Sun* every day to find out what happened next. No formal retraction appeared until weeks later, by which point circulation had jumped permanently.

The hoax's significance is structural rather than satirical. It was the first clean demonstration that fabricated news durably grew readership — even after exposure — and proved out the business case for fake news inside a mass-market commercial paper. Every subsequent yellow-journalism stunt, from Poe's balloon hoax to the *Maine* coverage, runs on the operating model the Moon Hoax established.
