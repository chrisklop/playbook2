Edward A. Pollard, editor of the Richmond *Examiner*, coined "Lost Cause" in 1866 to reframe Confederate defeat as a tragic but principled stand. The United Daughters of the Confederacy, founded in 1894, then industrialized the project. Their core claims — that the Civil War was about "states' rights" rather than slavery, that slavery was benevolent, and that enslaved people had been content — were drilled into Southern public memory across generations.

The UDC's distribution machinery was unusually disciplined for a memory campaign. It pushed monument construction in courthouse squares, ran school essay contests, sponsored lecture circuits, and — most consequentially — vetted history textbooks used across the South for decades, screening manuscripts for ideological compliance before they reached classrooms.

The Lost Cause is the era's clearest demonstration that slow, institutional, multi-decade disinformation outperforms any single news cycle. It also shows that memory engineering does not need a centralized state propaganda office; a coordinated civil-society network with access to schools, monuments, and curricula can rewrite a region's history within two generations.
