Édouard Drumont's antisemitic daily *La Libre Parole* broke the Dreyfus story on November 1, 1894 — "High Treason: Arrest of the Jewish Officer" — using leaked military material to frame the arrest of Jewish artillery captain Alfred Dreyfus as proof of an "international Jewish conspiracy." French General Staff officers had quietly fed the paper the leak; the conviction soon followed on forged evidence, the so-called *bordereau*.

The case became a years-long stress test of the modern press as a political weapon. Anonymous leaks from a state security apparatus were laundered through a partisan newspaper, then echoed across the rest of the political press, with conspiracy framing doing the connective tissue. Émile Zola's open letter "J'accuse…!" in *L'Aurore* on January 13, 1898 — 200,000 copies sold in Paris in a single day — finally cracked the official narrative, but the country remained split for a decade.

Dreyfus was eventually exonerated in 1906. The affair is the canonical demonstration of how secret-police forgery plus a friendly newspaper plus conspiracy framing can convict an innocent man and divide a nation — the same pattern that would reappear, scaled up, in the *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* just five years later.
