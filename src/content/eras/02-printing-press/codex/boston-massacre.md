Five colonists died in a chaotic confrontation with British soldiers on a snowy Boston night in March 1770. Within hours, Samuel Adams was branding it the "Boston Massacre" — a name that prejudged the question the upcoming criminal trials were supposed to decide.

Paul Revere then pirated a more accurate engraving by Henry Pelham, who had given him an advance look at the print. Revere's altered version showed the British soldiers firing a disciplined volley on direct command at peaceful citizens, omitting the snowballs, ice, and clubs the crowd had thrown. Captain Preston was depicted ordering the shots — a claim the trial would later reject. The engraving circulated through the colonies before the soldiers ever saw a courtroom.

The operation combined three modern techniques that would echo for centuries: branding-by-naming (the "Massacre"), targeted image manipulation, and what is essentially jury intimidation through saturating the relevant venue with prejudicial imagery — plus, as a bonus, the plagiarism of the original source artist who never got paid.
