Defrocked clergyman Titus Oates fabricated a Jesuit conspiracy to assassinate Charles II and install his Catholic brother James on the throne. Even after Charles personally caught Oates contradicting himself under questioning, the panic took on a life of its own.

The catalyst was the suspicious murder of magistrate Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey, who had taken Oates's sworn deposition. Godfrey's corpse — found impaled and bruised in a ditch — was instantly read as evidence the plot was real, providing a sympathetic martyr to launder Oates's invented testimony into national fact. Hundreds of thousands of pamphlets flooded London coffeehouses.

Twenty-two innocent Catholics were executed before the panic burned itself out. Oates was eventually convicted of perjury and pilloried. The case is a clean demonstration of how false witness, a well-timed real-world tragedy, pamphlet saturation, and latent prejudice can compound into mass judicial murder — even when the chief witness is already a known liar.
