From his print shop in Passy, outside Paris, Benjamin Franklin forged an entire fake issue of a real newspaper — a "Supplement to the Boston Independent Chronicle" — complete with masthead, plausible advertisements, and a fabricated dispatch from one "Captain Gerrish." The headline item described bales of American scalps, including women's and children's, taken by British-allied Native Americans and intended as a gift to the King.

Franklin quietly fed the forgery to John Adams, John Jay, and trusted couriers, laundering it into the British press during peace negotiations to harden European opinion against Britain's conduct of the war. The technique was clean: don't write a pamphlet attacking the enemy, become the enemy's own newspaper and let them attack themselves.

Roughly thirty-five American newspapers eventually reprinted the supplement as fact. Its forged origin was not definitively established until 1854 — more than seventy years later. Franklin's experiment is the prototype for every later fake-outlet impersonation campaign and the laundered-source amplification pattern that still defines state-sponsored disinformation today.
