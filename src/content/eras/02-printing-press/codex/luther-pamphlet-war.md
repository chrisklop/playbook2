Luther and Lucas Cranach the Elder ran arguably the first integrated branding-and-propaganda operation in European history. Short, vernacular pamphlets traveled with distinctive Wittenberg title-page woodcuts and savage visual satire — the Pope as Antichrist, monks rendered as devils, the so-called "Papal Ass." The combination of cheap text and visceral imagery let the message punch through to illiterate audiences in taverns and markets.

German presses produced over 7,000 pamphlet editions between 1520 and 1525 — more than double the previous decade. Saxony's small workshops became virally networked publishing nodes, reprinting Luther's tracts faster than Catholic authorities could suppress them.

The Reformation playbook — vernacular accessibility, visual emotion, brand identity, viral replication, and stark in-group/out-group polarization — became the template every confessional and political conflict for the next two centuries would copy.
