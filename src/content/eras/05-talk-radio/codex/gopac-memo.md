In 1990, **GOPAC** — the political action committee Newt Gingrich chaired — mailed a training document to thousands of Republican candidates for state and local office. Titled **"Language: A Key Mechanism of Control,"** it consisted almost entirely of two word lists.

**Words to use about your opponent**: *decay, sick, pathetic, lie, liberal, betray, traitor, criminal, destroy, sick, hypocrisy, anti-flag, anti-family, anti-jobs, radical, corrupt, intolerant.*

**Words to use about yourself**: *family, courage, dream, freedom, pristine, hard work, opportunity, principle, sincere, vision, courage, prosperity, environment, peace, learn, lead, building, fair.*

The memo's framing was instructional: "These are powerful words that can help you to develop a message and to identify yourself." There was no claim that the words were accurate descriptions. The point was repetition and saturation — that a candidate who used the same vocabulary as every other candidate in the network would, collectively, define the opposition's character before the opposition spoke.

The memo became one of the most-quoted documents in late-20th-century U.S. politics because it laid out, in a single page, a deliberate program of partisan vocabulary engineering. By the mid-1990s, the words from the "opponent" list ("sick," "pathetic," "traitor") were standard talk-radio phrasing — the language layer of the same wave of grievance media Limbaugh was running on the radio dial.
