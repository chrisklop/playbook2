Late Saturday night, **January 17, 1998**, at **11:32 PM Eastern**, the Drudge Report posted a 78-word item under the headline **"NEWSWEEK KILLS STORY ON WHITE HOUSE INTERN."** Drudge had been tipped that *Newsweek* reporter Michael Isikoff's investigation into President Clinton's relationship with a 24-year-old White House intern had been held by his editors. The post named the intern: Monica Lewinsky.

Within 36 hours the story was on every major American network. *Newsweek* posted Isikoff's article online the following Wednesday — by then the cable coverage had been continuous for days.

What this moment crystallized:

- A **single-operator website**, written and hosted by Matt Drudge from a one-bedroom apartment in Hollywood, could function as an upstream wire service for major broadcast outlets. The cable networks did not need to do the verification themselves; they covered Drudge's posting *as the story*.
- The **"Drudge effect"** — that a red-letter headline at the top of drudgereport.com could redirect that morning's cable news agenda — became a documented phenomenon studied by media scholars throughout the late 1990s and 2000s. Politico's 2007 analysis found roughly two-thirds of Drudge's lead links migrated to cable within hours.
- The site itself stayed deliberately primitive: black-on-white sans-serif, a single big headline, three columns of links, a flashing police-light GIF for emergencies. The aesthetic was the message — *unfiltered, unedited, just before press time.*

The Drudge Report is one of the cleanest historical examples of how a small editorial choice (which link sits at the top right now) can become a national agenda-setting mechanism when the downstream pipeline is built around it.
