The **Fairness Doctrine** was an FCC policy adopted in 1949 requiring broadcast license holders to (1) present controversial issues of public importance and (2) do so in a manner that was "honest, equitable, and balanced" — typically meaning that on-air opinion required on-air rebuttal.

On **August 4, 1987**, the FCC voted **4–0** to repeal it, finding that the doctrine "restricts the journalistic freedom of broadcasters" and was no longer needed in a market with cable, VCRs, and a growing media diversity. Congress passed a bill restoring the doctrine the same year; President Reagan vetoed it. A second attempt in 1991 was abandoned under threat of a Bush 41 veto.

The downstream effect was almost immediate. Rush Limbaugh's show — which had been a regional Sacramento program since 1984 — went into national syndication in **August 1988**, exactly one year after the repeal. By 1993 it was on 650+ stations. The format Limbaugh codified (three-hour daily monologue, callers as chorus, no obligation to host opposing views) became the template for hundreds of imitators across the AM band through the 1990s.

It is rare in U.S. media history to point at a single regulatory change and trace a coherent line to a partisan media ecosystem. This is one of those moments. The repeal didn't *create* talk radio — but it removed the structural obstacle that had kept the format off the air for forty years.
