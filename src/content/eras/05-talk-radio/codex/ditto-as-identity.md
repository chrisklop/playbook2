The Limbaugh show convention was that callers, on getting through, would say **"Dittos, Rush"** rather than recap their agreement with the previous caller. The shorthand spread to the audience itself: *Rush Limbaugh fans* became *Dittoheads*, a self-applied identity badge by the early 1990s.

What looks like a quirk of radio etiquette is actually a structural innovation.

- **Argument is replaced by ratification.** A caller does not have to demonstrate that they agree with the substance — only that they identify with the group. This shifts the work of persuasion from each broadcast to the act of tuning in.
- **The audience polices itself.** Dittohead identity created strong in-group pressure to consume the same media and to repeat the same framings. Listeners stopped arguing with the host and started arguing with each other *on behalf of* the host.
- **The host's worldview is exempt from interrogation.** A two-hour monologue with no rebuttal segments is structurally different from any prior radio format. Combined with caller-as-chorus, the host's frame becomes the only frame the audience ever practices articulating.

This pattern recurs in every subsequent disinformation ecosystem the game tracks. The chyron, the Q-drop, the YouTube comment thread, the X reply guy — all are variations on the same move: *make agreement the price of admission, then sell agreement as community.* Limbaugh wasn't the inventor of the technique, but he was the first U.S. broadcaster to wire it into a national daily audience, and the template has been imitated continuously since.
