In the photographic archive of any totalitarian state, people disappear at the speed of the political need. The Soviet Union produced the most extensive documented case. Throughout the **1920s, 30s, and 40s**, **Glavlit** (the State Publishing House's censorship office) and *Pravda*'s photo desk systematically retouched, cropped, and airbrushed official photographs to remove figures who had fallen out of favor — usually because they had been arrested, executed, or "vanished" during the **Great Purges (1936–1938)** or earlier factional struggles.

The most-reproduced single case is **NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov**, photographed walking beside Stalin along the Moscow-Volga Canal in 1937. Yezhov had personally directed the Purges' peak killings. By 1940 he had himself been arrested and shot. The photograph was reprinted — with **Yezhov replaced by canal water**. Newspaper editors and schoolteachers across the USSR were instructed to remove him from prior printings; some literally cut figures out of textbooks with scissors and razors.

British historian and graphic designer **David King** spent thirty years collecting Soviet originals and their successive retouched versions. His *The Commissar Vanishes* (1997) reproduces hundreds of side-by-side pairs: Lenin photographed with Trotsky, then with Trotsky scraped out; the leadership of the 1917 Revolution disappearing year by year as Stalin consolidated power. The collection is now at the **Tate Modern**.

The companion technique was **epistemological**: under **Trofim Lysenko** (1898–1976), the regime denied Mendelian genetics in favor of a Lamarckian ideology of inheritance. After the **August 1948 VASKhNIL session**, classical genetics was effectively outlawed in the USSR; ~3,000 biologists were dismissed, imprisoned, or executed; Soviet agricultural science collapsed for decades. Where photographic falsification rewrote *who had existed*, Lysenkoism rewrote *what was true about the natural world*.

The Soviet method was eventually catalogued because the regime was both rigorous and bureaucratic enough to preserve copies of its own falsifications. Digital tools have since made the gesture vastly cheaper, faster, harder to trace, and entirely deniable — but the conceptual move is exactly the one Glavlit standardized: erase the inconvenient and reprint the past as if it had always been the way the present requires it to be.
