**Edward Bernays** (1891–1995), a veteran of the **Creel Committee** and Sigmund Freud's nephew, was retained in 1928 by **George Washington Hill**, president of the American Tobacco Company, for a single-objective campaign: break the social taboo against women smoking in public. Lucky Strike's domestic market had stalled at the male half of the country.

Bernays consulted the psychoanalyst **A.A. Brill**, who advised him to frame cigarettes for women as a feminist gesture — "torches of freedom" — that defied the masculine monopoly on public smoking. Bernays then **pre-tipped a list of journalists** to be at the **New York City Easter Sunday Parade on March 31, 1929**, and recruited a group of well-known debutantes (organized through *Vogue*'s society writer Bertha Hunt) to march down Fifth Avenue lighting Lucky Strikes "at the appropriate signal." The march itself was unannounced and presented as spontaneous. The next day's newspapers carried the photographs and the framing intact.

Within a year, women's share of US cigarette consumption rose from ~5% (1923) to ~12% (1929); within two decades it would reach ~33%. American Tobacco's stock followed the same curve.

The Torches of Freedom is the **founding template of modern PR**: a manufactured pseudo-event, designed and orchestrated to *appear* spontaneous, that generates apparently independent news coverage. Bernays codified the method explicitly in *Propaganda* (1928) and *Public Relations* (1952). His clients across forty years included United Fruit (whose 1954 PR campaign shaped the US-backed coup against Guatemala's Árbenz), the American Beet Sugar Association, Procter & Gamble, and three sitting presidents. Asked late in life whether he regretted any of his work, he answered: only the cigarettes. He hadn't known they killed people.

The line from Bernays' Easter parade to every contemporary "viral moment" carefully staged for the press is direct and unbroken. He more or less invented the work.
