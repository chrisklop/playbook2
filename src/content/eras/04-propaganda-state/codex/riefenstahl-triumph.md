Premiered **March 28, 1935** at the Berlin Ufa-Palast am Zoo, in Hitler's presence. The film documented the **6th NSDAP Party Congress** held at Nuremberg in September 1934 — itself partially **staged for the camera**. Albert Speer designed the rally architecture (the "Cathedral of Light" was 152 anti-aircraft searchlights aimed vertically) specifically knowing it would be filmed. The pseudo-event predated the documentation; the documentation manufactured a deeper pseudo-event.

Director **Leni Riefenstahl** commanded 172 crew members, 30 cameras, four sound trucks, and an unprecedented budget (~7 million Reichsmarks, ~$2.5 million in 1935 USD). The technical innovations she deployed became the visual grammar of state propaganda for the next century:

- **Low-angle hero shots** (Hitler photographed from below, sky behind him)
- **Aerial cinematography** (helicopter-borne shots over rally formations)
- **Tracking shots on rails and elevators** (rising past Speer's columns at the Zeppelinfeld)
- **Choral music-image montage** (Herbert Windt's score cut to march tempo)
- **Anonymous mass composition** (uniformed crowds rendered as geometric texture, individual faces rare and chosen)

Frank Capra's later *Why We Fight* series (1942–1945) cut Riefenstahl's footage against Allied narration to **indict the techniques it demonstrated** — a kind of black-propaganda jiu-jitsu, using the regime's own aesthetic legibility against it. *Prelude to War* (1942) won the 1943 Academy Award for Best Documentary using sequences sourced from *Triumph*.

The film's afterlife is its real subject. Riefenstahl spent the rest of her long life (she lived to 101) insisting she had been "merely a filmmaker." Most film historians disagree: the formal mastery is precisely what made it propaganda's monumental template. Susan Sontag's "Fascinating Fascism" (*New York Review of Books*, 1975) remains the standard rebuttal — that the aesthetic IS the argument, and that the elegance was never separable from the politics.
