President Wilson created the Committee on Public Information by Executive Order 2594 on **April 13, 1917**, one week after the US declaration of war. Journalist George Creel ran it. By 1919 it had produced ~75 million pamphlets, coordinated 1,500 commissioned posters, fed ~6,000 press releases to a newspaper industry that printed them as straight news, and ran the first systematic foreign-language outreach to immigrant communities.

The most striking innovation was the **Four-Minute Men**: ~75,000 volunteer orators who, between reels at cinemas across 5,200 American communities, delivered short scripted speeches on themes the CPI distributed weekly. Over 750,000 speeches reached an estimated 400 million listener-instances. It is the first total-spectrum domestic propaganda campaign in democratic history — and its veterans (Bernays, Lippmann, Byoir) carried the techniques directly into commercial public relations after the Armistice.

Creel's own memoir *How We Advertised America* (1920) treated the bureau as a triumph. Lippmann, who had worked alongside him in military intelligence, used *Public Opinion* (1922) to argue something darker: that mass democracies cannot function without permanent expert-managed opinion. The CPI was disbanded; the doctrine that made it possible was not.
