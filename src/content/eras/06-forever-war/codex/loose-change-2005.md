**Loose Change** was a feature-length 9/11 conspiracy documentary written and directed by **Dylan Avery**, age 22, on a modest budget — originally as a planned fiction film, then re-cut into a documentary when the source material was determined to be more "interesting." The first version was released in April 2005, the second in November 2005, and the substantially-expanded **"Loose Change: Final Cut"** in November 2007.

The film was distributed primarily by file-sharing, then YouTube, then a small DVD pressing run. **By 2006 it had been downloaded or streamed more than 100 million times**; *Vanity Fair*, in a 2006 profile, called it "the first internet blockbuster." Estimates of cumulative views by 2010 ranged from 1 to 2.2 billion.

The film's editorial strategy is the historically important part:

- **No explicit claim is unfalsifiable.** Each segment poses a question — *Why does this footage look unusual? How did Building 7 fall the way it did? Why was the Pentagon damage that shape?* — and lets the viewer answer.
- **Every fact cited is, in isolation, true** (or close to true): yes, Building 7 did fall, yes, NORAD's response time was anomalous, yes, war games were running that morning. The film's editorial work is in the **juxtaposition**, not in any single claim.
- **The visual grammar is borrowed from skeptic documentaries** the audience already trusts (anti-Iraq-war montages, Discovery Channel forensics). The form is news; the content is innuendo.

This is the **"just asking questions"** technique formalised at scale. It does not assert; it implies. The viewer arrives at the conspiratorial conclusion themselves, which produces stronger conviction than being told.

The downstream consequence:

- **A 2008 Scripps-Howard survey** found 36% of U.S. respondents thought it was *"very likely"* or *"somewhat likely"* the U.S. government was involved in the 9/11 attacks. That number had been ~6% in late 2001.
- **The film's editorial structure was directly imitated** by later viral conspiracy media — *Zeitgeist* (2007), *Plandemic* (2020), the QAnon "drops" — all of which use the same *"here's a question, here's a juxtaposition, you decide"* grammar.

What **Loose Change** demonstrated, before YouTube was monetizing and before "fact-check" was a search term, is that **a coherent conspiratorial worldview can be assembled from true facts arranged in a particular order**, with no false claim necessary. The technique is now standard.
