**Swift Boat Veterans for Truth** was a 527 group formed in May 2004 to oppose John Kerry's presidential campaign by attacking his Vietnam service record. The political playbook that followed gave English a new verb: *to swift-boat* — to attack a documented credential by inventing a counter-narrative that uses the credentialed person's own record as the surface area of attack.

The mechanics:

- **John Kerry served on Swift Boats in the Mekong Delta in 1968–69.** He was awarded the Silver Star, the Bronze Star, and three Purple Hearts. The medals and the citation records were public.
- **Swift Boat Veterans for Truth ran ads through August–October 2004** in which other former Navy veterans claimed (against the contemporaneous medal citations and the on-record testimony of every officer who had served with Kerry) that the events leading to his awards had been embellished or fabricated.
- **None of the veterans in the ads had served on Kerry's boats.** The closest contemporaneous critic, John O'Neill (co-founder of the group), had commanded a Swift Boat in the same area months after Kerry's tour.
- **Roughly $22 million** in ad spend across August and September 2004 — modest by general-election standards. The leverage came from network news coverage of the *controversy*, which delivered roughly $200–300 million worth of equivalent earned-media impressions.

By Election Day, **57% of Americans surveyed told pollsters they had heard "a lot" about the Swift Boat claims.** Of those, a majority answered uncertainly when asked whether Kerry's service record was credible. Kerry lost Ohio by 118,000 votes — and the campaign's internal post-mortem judged the Swift Boat attack to have been the single most-damaging surface in the entire race.

The lasting template:

1. **Attack the strongest credential, not the weakest.** A documented record produces a larger surface for innuendo than a known weakness.
2. **Use proximate-but-uninvolved sources.** O'Neill's Swift Boat service in the same waters in a different period was enough credibility for cable bookers, even when his testimony contradicted every officer who had actually served with Kerry.
3. **Don't refute; insinuate.** The ads' phrasing was almost entirely *"we cannot find evidence that"* and *"we do not believe."* The Sullivan defamation standard was structurally protected.
4. **Win the news cycle, not the lawsuit.** Most Swift Boat claims were retracted, withdrawn, or fact-checked into dust by November 2004. The retractions did not catch the original ads' reach by a factor of ten.

The term has since been applied to the equivalent move against any documented record — Joe Biden's son's business activities, Hillary Clinton's State Department service, Tim Walz's National Guard service. The 2004 campaign codified the technique.
